Key definitions were established through a real-world scenario involving New York City residents' spending habits, followed by a collaborative review of practice examples to prepare for the upcoming exam.

Statistical Fundamentals and Vocabulary

  • The instructor emphasized that modern statistics focuses on interpretation and analysis rather than accurate manual calculation, as technology now handles the latter.
  • Target population consists of all elements, individuals, or objects whose characteristics are being studied.
  • Parameter is a numerical value that describes a population characteristic.
  • Sample is a subset or portion of the population selected for study.
  • Statistic is a numerical value that describes a sample characteristic.
  • Sampling variability refers to the fact that observed values of a statistic vary depending on the particular sample selected from the population.
  • Population size is represented by capital N, while sample size is represented by lowercase n.
  • A parameter is a constant (a unique, often unknown value), whereas a statistic is a variable (changes from sample to sample).

Case Study: NYC Clothing Expenditures

  • The group analyzed a hypothetical study of the average amount spent on clothing by all residents in New York City over the past three months.
  • The target population is all residents in NYC (approximately 8 million people).
  • A sample size of 10,000 residents was selected randomly to estimate the population average.
  • Random selection is critical for reliability; using ZIP codes and ratios helps ensure the sample is representative of the entire city.
  • The discussion highlighted that a single sample statistic (e.g., $782) might not be a perfectly reliable estimate of the population parameter because outliers (billionaires or homeless individuals) can skew data.
  • Sampling variability was demonstrated by showing how different samples of 10,000 people could yield different averages (e.g., $563 vs. $1,000+).

Practice Exercises and Exam Preparation

  • Example 3: It is considered true that it is realistically impossible for the Census Bureau to obtain data for every single person in the US due to the sheer volume and accessibility of residents.

Key outcomes included defining categorical and quantitative variables, distinguishing between discrete and continuous data, and identifying appropriate graphical tools for data visualization.

Variable Classification

  • Categorical (Qualitative) Variables: Describe qualities or categories and can be represented numerically (e.g., ID, Student ID, Employee ID, Driver's license, Social security number, Passport number, ZIP code, Bank account number) or non-numerically (e.g., major, employment status, birth month).
  • Quantitative (Numerical) Variables: Must have mathematical meaning where totals or averages make sense.
    • Discrete: Items that are counted (e.g., number of siblings, number of customers, number of buildings, number of bank accounts).
    • Continuous: Items that are measured (e.g., height, weight, distance, salary/income, temperature, length of time).
  • Note on Overlap: Some variables like age or monetary value are technically continuous but are often treated as discrete for practical purposes.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

  • Definition: A "baby step" analysis to evaluate data quality, clean errors (e.g., impossible ages or GPAs), and identify data types before complex modeling.
  • Graphical Approach: Used for both categorical and quantitative variables to identify characteristics.
  • Descriptive Approach: Used strictly for quantitative variables to find summary statistics (mean, median, variation).

Data Visualization Tools

  • Categorical Graphs:
    • Bar graph (best for many categories).
    • Pie chart (best for few categories).
  • Quantitative Graphs:
    • Histogram.
    • Dot plot.
    • Box plot (Box and Whisker plot) - to be covered in Chapter 4.
    • Stem and leaf plot.
  • Time Series Analysis: Used for data like historical stock prices (beyond the scope of basic EDA).
  • Linear Regression: Analyzing correlation between two quantitative variables (Chapter 5).

Frequency Distributions

  • Frequency: The count of observations in a category.
  • Relative Frequency: The proportion of observations (Frequency / Total Frequency).
  • Mutually Exclusive Categories: In the "Where are you from?" example, a student cannot be from two categories simultaneously.
    • "New York State AND Other Country" = 0.
    • "New York State OR Other Country" = Sum of both frequencies.

📊 Understanding Variation: Range, Standard Deviation & More

Complete lesson: from two‑student extremes to average spread, calculation steps, interpretation, and notation

📏 Range vs. Standard Deviation

Range = highest score − lowest score. It's quick but uses only two values (min & max) and ignores all other data points. In a class of 35 students, range ignores 33 students' performance.

✅ Advantage of range: Extremely easy to compute.
❌ Disadvantage: Ignores the whole distribution. Not reliable when data have similar range but different internal spread.

Standard deviation approximates the typical (average) distance between each data point and the mean. It answers: “On average, how many points does each student's score differ from the class average?”

🔑 Key relationship: You cannot compute standard deviation without first calculating the mean. They are “best friends” — almost inseparable. Mean first, then deviation from the mean.
🏢 Company age example — Why range isn’t enough

Three companies, each with 6 randomly selected employees (sample data). All have the same mean and median (45 years) and symmetric graphs. Companies 2 & 3 have identical range = 50 years, but the internal spread differs.

Company 1

Range = 10 years

Ages: 40, 42, 44, 46, 48, 50
Min=40, Max=50

Company 2

Range = 50 years

Ages: 20, 30, 45, 45, 60, 70
Min=20, Max=70

Company 3

Range = 50 years

Ages: 20, 42, 44, 46, 48, 70
Min=20, Max=70

Observation: Companies 2 and 3 have identical range (50) and identical mean/median (45). Yet their age distributions are not the same. Range alone cannot distinguish between them. This proves range is not satisfactory for comparing variability.

📐 Three steps to calculate sample standard deviation

Using Company 3 ages: 20, 42, 44, 46, 48, 70. Mean = 45 years.

1 Find the sample mean (x̄)
x̄ = (Σx) / n = (20+42+44+46+48+70)/6 = 270/6 = 45 years
2 Calculate sample variance (s²)

Deviations: (20−45)=−25, (42−45)=−3, (44−45)=−1, (46−45)=+1, (48−45)=+3, (70−45)=+25.
Sum of raw deviations = 0 (always). So we square each deviation.

s² = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) = (625+9+1+1+9+625) / 5 = 1270 / 5 = 254 (squared years)
⚠️ Variance has no practical real-world unit (squared years) — it's an intermediate mathematical step.
3 Take square root → Sample standard deviation (s)
s = √(s²) = √254 ≈ 15.94 years
🎯 Interpretation: On average, each employee's age differs from the mean (45 years) by about 15.94 years.
📖 Interpreting standard deviation & comparing companies

🔍 What does s = 15.9 (Company 2) or s = 18.7 (Company 3) tell us?

For Company 3, standard deviation ≈ 18.7 years means: on average, each person's age differs from 45 by about 18.7 years. Some much younger, some older, but after averaging those differences, that's the typical deviation.

📊 Company 2 (actual)

s ≈ 15.9

Ages: 20,30,45,45,60,70
Most values cluster near 45 → smaller spread.

📈 Company 3 (actual)

s ≈ 18.7

Ages: 20,42,44,46,48,70
More evenly spread from mean → larger SD.

❓ Why is Company 2's standard deviation smaller?
Because most ages are very close to 45 (two values exactly at 45, others 30 and 60 moderate). Company 3 has intermediate ages (42,44,46,48) creating larger overall dispersion.
💹 Standard deviation in finance (risk & volatility)

In finance, standard deviation measures volatility — the risk of an investment. Two stocks may have the same expected return (mean), but the one with larger price swings has a larger standard deviation (higher risk).

🧓 Investment note: When young, higher risk (larger SD) may be acceptable. As you get older, you prefer investments with lower standard deviation → less uncertainty and lower risk.

📌 Extreme case: Standard deviation = 0

Standard deviation can be zero — the lowest possible value. This means no spread at all; all data points are exactly the same. (e.g., every employee age = 45). Variance and standard deviation are always ≥ 0.

📚 Vocabulary: spread, variability, variation, dispersion
📈 Spread 📉 Variability 🔄 Variation 🎯 Dispersion 📊 Volatility (finance)

These terms are used interchangeably when describing how data scatters. However, "deviation" specifically refers to the difference between a data point and the mean (xᵢ − x̄). Variation/variability are broader statistical concepts but essentially mean the same thing: the amount of spread in a dataset.

✍️ Notation: Sample vs. Population

📌 Sample statistics

  • x̄ (x-bar) → sample mean
  • s² → sample variance
  • s or sₓ → sample standard deviation

🌍 Population parameters (Greek)

  • μ (mu) → population mean
  • σ² (sigma squared) → population variance
  • σ (sigma) → population standard deviation
🔁 √(variance) = standard deviation    (for both sample and population)

Whenever you take the square root of variance, you obtain standard deviation — regardless of whether it's a sample (s) or population (σ).

🧠 Complete takeaway:
• Range is quick but ignores most data.
• Standard deviation uses all values and the mean — average distance from the mean.
• Calculation path: Mean → deviations → squared deviations → variance → square root → SD.
• Larger SD = more spread; SD = 0 → no variation.
• Spread, variability, dispersion, volatility all refer to the same core idea.
• Sample notation: x̄, s², s  |  Population notation: μ, σ², σ.

Overview

The session covered core probability ideas using coin-toss examples (fair vs. unfair), translating outcomes into fractions/decimals/percentages, multiplying probabilities across independent trials, and using complements to compute “at least one” probabilities. It concluded with applying the same logic to a real-world train-track stopping problem across a workweek.

Coin toss probability (fair coin)

  • For 3 tosses of a fair coin, any specific ordered outcome (e.g., H-T-H) has probability 1/8 = 12.5%.
  • “Exactly one tail” in 3 tosses has 3 favorable outcomes (T-H-H, H-T-H, H-H-T) out of 8:
    • Probability = 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%.
  • Notation/representation caution:
    • 0.375% is incorrect when the correct probability is 37.5% (or 0.375).

Multiplying probabilities across tosses (fair coin)

  • Independent-trial multiplication shown via repeated “take half again”:
    • P(H) = 50%
    • P(HH) = 50% of 50% = 25%
    • P(HHH) = 50% of 25% = 12.5%
    • P(HHHH) = 50% of 12.5% = 6.25%
  • Conceptual emphasis: for a fair coin, each additional required head multiplies by 1/2 again.

Unfair coin examples (e.g., P(H)=80%)

  • If the coin is unfair with P(H)=80% and P(T)=20%:
    • P(H) = 80%
    • P(HH) = 80% of 80% = 64%
    • P(HHH) = 80% of 64% = 51.2%
  • Mixed-sequence examples:
    • P(HHT) = 20% of 64% = 12.8% (equivalently 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.2)
    • P(HHTT) = 20% of 12.8% = 2.56% (equivalently 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.2 × 0.2)
  • Generalization discussed:
    • If P(H)=40%, then P(HH)=40% of 40% = 16%, and further multiplications continue similarly (using 40% for H, 60% for T).

Probability properties and complement rule

  • Probability bounds:
    • Each event probability must be between 0% and 100% (equivalently between 0 and 1).
  • Total probability across all outcomes:
    • Probabilities of all outcomes in the full sample space must sum to 100% (1).
  • Complement concept:
    • If P(A)=30% (rain tomorrow), then P(AC)=70% (no rain tomorrow).
    • Complement is treated as binary “A vs not A” (discussion indicated complements are not “multiple events all complementing each other”).
  • “At least one” via complement:
    • For 3 fair coin tosses: P(at least one head) = 1 - P(no heads).
    • P(no heads) = P(TTT) = 1/8 = 12.5%.
    • So P(at least one head) = 7/8 = 87.5%.
    • Long way: count 7 winning outcomes out of 8; quick way: 100% - 12.5%.

Train-track stopping problem (weekday workweek)

  • Setup:
    • Probability of getting stopped on a given weekday = 15%.
    • Probability of not getting stopped = 85% (complement).
  • Part A (stopped Monday and Tuesday):
    • P(stop Mon and Tue) = 15% × 15% = 2.25%.
  • Clarification made during discussion:
    • “Every day” means every weekday (Mon-Fri).

1. Mutually Exclusive vs. Overlapping Events

  • Mutually exclusive: Events cannot happen at the same time.
    • Example: Being employed and unemployed at the same time is impossible → probability = 0.
    • Birthday in May and June for the same person → impossible.
  • Overlapping (not mutually exclusive): Events can occur together.
    • Example: Being employed and having a bachelor's degree → 32 people fall into both categories.

2. The Addition Rule for "Or" Probabilities

When calculating P(A or B), subtract the overlap to avoid double-counting.

Formula:
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)

Example (Employment & Degree Table)

  • Total people = 80
  • Employed = 65
  • Bachelor's degree = 41
  • Employed and bachelor's = 32

P(Employed or Bachelor's) = 65/80 + 41/80 - 32/80 = 74/80

Why subtract? The 32 people in both groups would otherwise be counted twice.

3. Conditional Probability ("Given")

The word "given" restricts the group you're looking at - it becomes your new denominator.

Formula:
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Examples

  • P(bachelor's | employed):
    • Denominator = employed (65)
    • Numerator = employed & bachelor's (32)
    • Result = 32/65
  • P(unemployed | master's):
    • Denominator = master's (27)
    • Numerator = unemployed & master's (4)
    • Result = 4/27

4. Multiplication Rule & Bayes' Rule

General Multiplication Rule:
P(A and B) = P(A | B) × P(B)
Bayes' Rule:
P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)

Bayes' Rule is useful when you need to "reverse" a conditional probability.

5. Color-Blindness Example (Step-by-Step)

Given:

  • 50% male, 50% female
  • P(color-blind | male) = 5% → P(normal | male) = 95%
  • P(color-blind | female) = 1% → P(normal | female) = 99%

Joint Probabilities (overall population):

  • Color-blind male: 0.50 × 0.05 = 2.5%
  • Normal vision male: 0.50 × 0.95 = 47.5%
  • Color-blind female: 0.50 × 0.01 = 0.5%
  • Normal vision female: 0.50 × 0.99 = 49.5%

Questions & Answers:

  • A) P(male) = 50%
  • B) P(color-blind and male) = 2.5%
  • C) P(color-blind) = 2.5% + 0.5% = 3%
  • D) P(male | color-blind) = 2.5% / 3% ≈ 83.3%
  • E) P(female | normal vision) =
    • Denominator = P(normal vision) = 97%
    • Numerator = P(female and normal) = 49.5%
    • Result = 49.5% / 97% ≈ 51.0%

Summary of Key Formulas

  • Addition Rule: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A ∩ B)
  • Complement: P(A') = 1 - P(A)
  • Multiplication Rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
  • Conditional Probability: P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
  • Bayes' Rule: P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)
  • Exactly one event occurs: P(exactly one) = P(A ∪ B) - P(A ∩ B)

Summary: Discrete Probability Distribution

Context

We're looking at a scenario where we select 3 people on Black Friday. Each person has a 70% chance of buying something (and a 30% chance of not buying).

Step 1: List All Possible Outcomes & Probabilities

Each outcome is a combination of 3 people (B = buys, N = doesn't buy):

  • BBB (all 3 buy): 0.70 × 0.70 × 0.70 = 34.3%
  • BBN (first two buy, third doesn't): 0.70 × 0.70 × 0.30 = 14.7%
  • ... and so on for all 8 possible outcomes (23 = 8).
  • NNN (nobody buys): 0.30 × 0.30 × 0.30 = 2.7%

Total probability = sum of all outcomes = 1.0 (100%) ✓

Step 2: What is a Discrete Probability Distribution?

A discrete probability distribution lists:

  • X = each possible value (e.g., number of buyers: 0, 1, 2, or 3)
  • P(X) = probability associated with each X value

Step 3: Two Key Properties of a Valid Probability Distribution

  1. Each probability P(X) must be between 0 and 1 (0% to 100%).
  2. The sum of all probabilities must equal 1 (100%).

Step 4: Finding the Mean (Expected Value)

The mean (or expected value) of a discrete random variable is found using:

µ = Σ [ X × P(X) ]

This is the theoretical average - what you'd expect on average over many repetitions. It's also called the population mean.

Example: Multiply each X value (0, 1, 2, 3) by its probability, then add them all up.

Step 5: Finding the Standard Deviation

There is also a formula to find the standard deviation (spread) of the variable X using the probability distribution. (Not fully detailed in the transcript, but it follows from the mean.)

Key Takeaways

  • Discrete probability distribution = table of all possible X values and their probabilities.
  • Properties: Each probability between 0 and 1; total = 1.
  • Mean (Expected Value): µ = Σ [ X × P(X) ] - the long-run average.
  • Standard deviation measures variability around the mean.

Chapter 7: Discrete Random Variables & Expected Value

1. Expected Value (Theoretical Average)

The expected value (or theoretical mean) of a discrete random variable \(X\) is the long-run average value after many repetitions. It is denoted by \(E(X)\) or \(\mu\).

\[ \mu = E(X) = \sum \left[ x \cdot P(x) \right] \]

This is the same formula as the weighted average. While the formula is the same, the interpretation differs: the expected value is a theoretical long-run average.

Example: Black Friday Customers

Let \(X\) = number of customers (out of 3) who buy something on Black Friday. The probability distribution is given.

\(x\)\(P(x)\)\(x \cdot P(x)\)
00.0270
10.1890.189
20.4410.882
30.3431.029

\[ \mu = 0 + 0.189 + 0.882 + 1.029 = 2.1 \]

Interpretation: On average, we expect 2.1 customers out of 3 to buy something.

Example: Coffee Machine Breakdowns

Let \(X\) = number of breakdowns per week. The probability distribution is:

\(x\)\(P(x)\)\(x \cdot P(x)\)
00.200
10.150.15
20.250.50
30.401.20

\[ \mu = 0 + 0.15 + 0.50 + 1.20 = 1.85 \]

Interpretation: The coffee machine is expected to break down about 1.85 times per week (approximately twice).

2. Standard Deviation of a Discrete Random Variable

The standard deviation measures the spread of the distribution. The formula is:

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{ \sum \left[ x^2 \cdot P(x) \right] - \mu^2 } \]

Steps:

  1. Compute \(x^2 \cdot P(x)\) for each value.
  2. Sum these products: \(\sum x^2 P(x)\).
  3. Subtract \(\mu^2\).
  4. Take the square root.

Example: Black Friday (continued)

\(x\)\(P(x)\)\(x^2 \cdot P(x)\)
00.0270
10.1890.189
20.4411.764
30.3433.087

\[ \sum x^2 P(x) = 0 + 0.189 + 1.764 + 3.087 = 5.04 \]

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{5.04 - (2.1)^2} = \sqrt{5.04 - 4.41} = \sqrt{0.63} \approx 0.79 \]

Note: Do not forget to take the square root. Without it, you get the variance.

3. Binomial Distribution (Brief Introduction)

When a discrete random variable satisfies the four binomial conditions, it follows a binomial distribution. The probability of exactly \(x\) successes in \(n\) trials is given by the binomial probability density function (PDF).

Technology (e.g., TI-83/84) can compute these probabilities using binompdf(n, p, x) or by generating a table of all possible \(x\) values.

Example: Black Friday (Binomial)

Here, \(n = 3\), \(p = 0.70\). The binomial PDF gives the same probabilities as the table:

  • \(P(X=0) = 0.027\)
  • \(P(X=1) = 0.189\)
  • \(P(X=2) = 0.441\)
  • \(P(X=3) = 0.343\)

4. The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule)

For a bell-shaped (normal) distribution, the following approximate percentages hold:

  • 68% of the data fall within \(\mu \pm 1\sigma\).
  • 95% of the data fall within \(\mu \pm 2\sigma\).
  • 99.7% of the data fall within \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\).

Example: IQ Scores

Suppose IQ scores are normally distributed with mean \(\mu = 100\) and standard deviation \(\sigma = 10\).

  • 68% of scores are between 90 and 110.
  • 95% of scores are between 80 and 120.
  • 99.7% of scores are between 70 and 130.

Example: Exam Scores

Exam scores are normally distributed with \(\mu = 80\) and \(\sigma = 5\).

Question: What is the probability that a randomly selected student scores higher than 80?

Answer: Since the distribution is symmetric, about 50% score above the mean.

Note: For continuous random variables, \(P(X > 80)\) is the same as \(P(X \geq 80)\) because the probability at a single point is zero. This is not the case for discrete variables.

Key Takeaways

  • Expected Value: \(E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(x)\) is the theoretical long-run average.
  • Standard Deviation (Discrete): \(\sigma = \sqrt{\sum x^2 P(x) - \mu^2}\).
  • Binomial Distribution: A special discrete distribution for counting successes in a fixed number of trials.
  • Normal Distribution: A continuous, bell-shaped distribution. The empirical rule gives quick probability estimates.
  • For continuous variables, \(P(X > a) = P(X \geq a)\).
